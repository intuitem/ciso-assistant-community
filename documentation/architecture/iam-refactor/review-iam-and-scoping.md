# IAM & scoping — object visibility specification

- **Status:** specification, ready for implementation decomposition. §1–§10 are normative; §11 (release plan) evolves with implementation; §12 (principles) is explanatory and binds nothing.
- **Date:** 2026-08-23
- **Supersedes:** `documentation/architecture/decisions/is-published-field-removal.md`
- **Complements:** `documentation/architecture/iam-refactor/iam-target-architecture-plan.md` (Phases 0–4 unchanged; this document replaces its Phase 5 and **pulls one of its cross-cutting deliverables forward into release 1** as platform hygiene — see §4.1 on `check_iam_closure`).

---

## Executive summary

**What this document is.** The specification for removing CISO Assistant's `is_published` mechanism and replacing it with plain RBAC, plus the two-release plan to ship it (§11).

**The problem.** Catalogs, shared vocabularies, and policies must be visible from sub-domains without a role assignment there. The original intent was simple — *everything is shared except assessments* — but the `is_published` boolean that implemented it drifted away from that: it sat on 158 models with per-model defaults that disagreed, no UI ever exposed it, and some access paths ignored it.

**The solution — no new mechanism.** Ambient visibility becomes ordinary RBAC data:

- Each folder has a **member group** (`<F> - members`) exactly while it grants its members a **default role** — every active internal user holding a role in the folder's branch, computed live by the IAM engine, never stored or synchronized. Third parties, service accounts, and enclave visitors are never included.
- One folder control, **one value**: the folder's **default role** — what its members may do on the folder itself. Setting it brings the member group into existence; clearing it removes it. Per-object sharing = move the objects to a shelf sub-folder and grant that sub-folder to the intended readers through its ordinary IAM groups.
- Three tenant-immutable built-in roles fill that control: **Reader Catalog** (the organization's reference material — the fresh-install default at Global), **Reader Migration (legacy)** (frozen at what `is_published` published in practice, assigned by the migration and expected to be traded in), and **Reader** (everything readable). A custom role covers anything narrower; empty means no ambient visibility. A **fresh install ships exactly one row** — `Global - members × Reader Catalog` — while a **migrated tenant receives `Reader Migration (legacy)` on Global and on every eligible folder** (§6.2). Either way, **at most one default-role row per folder**, and Global always carries it.
- The resolver keeps **zero visibility-specific rules**: every access question remains `is_access_allowed(permission, folder)`.

**The migration — land on today's reach, disclose the difference, then let tenants choose.** Release 2 applies `Reader Migration (legacy)` — a role shaped from what `is_published` published in practice — wherever it has an effect, and reports exhaustively, per folder and per model family, what each principal gains or loses. The remaining differences are structural, not accidental: a role grants per model where the flag published per object, and the per-user filtering `main` applied is gone. Moving to `Reader Catalog` afterwards is one dropdown.

**The plan (§11).** **Release 1:** the independent bug fixes (actor pickers §7.1, GlobalSettings permissions §7.2, the Reader gap §7.3) and the **IAM audit** tool under Extra → Experimental — the diff report, which remains afterwards as a permanent IAM audit feature. It creates no grants. **Release 2:** the member-group machinery and the migration that applies the posture and drops all 158 columns, atomically.

## 1. Problem statement

CISO Assistant has a valuable concept: some objects are visible from sub-domains without needing a role assignment there. Catalogs, shared vocabularies, and policies would be unusable without it. **We keep this concept.**

The concept had a clear original intent: **the organization's reference material is shared with everyone who works here; work in progress is not.** This specification expresses that as a role — `Reader Catalog` (§4.2) — rather than as a flag. An earlier formulation, *everything except assessments*, was rejected as unusable: its boundary is contested on every new model, whereas "is this reference material?" has an obvious answer nearly every time.

Its implementation — the `is_published` boolean — failed to express it, for four reasons:

1. It existed on **158 models**, including ones where publication is meaningless (components, entitlements, settings) and ones where it must never be off (frameworks, labels).
2. The intent was encoded as per-model boilerplate on a `default=False` base, so it drifted, with **four different kinds of writer**: **seventeen models opt back in** with `default=True` (the catalog vocabulary, the whole operational family, Comment); directory rows are forced published in `save()`; imported catalog objects are forced by the loader; and `PublishInRootFolderMixin` (`iam/models.py`) publishes **any of twenty-one models on save if its folder is the root** — labels, terminologies, asset classes, classification schemes, custom-field definitions, responsibility roles, entities, metric instances, organisation issues and objectives, risk acceptances, the threat-intelligence referentials (`Threat`, `CWE`, `Technique`, `SecurityAdvisory`), and most of the operational family. (An MRO closure returns twenty-two: the twenty-one declaring models plus `Policy`, a proxy of `AppliedControl` that inherits the behaviour without declaring it.) That fourth writer is the structural one, and thematically the closest thing on `main` to what this specification ships: it makes **placement itself a publishing act**. Meanwhile documents are published only by inheriting a policy's flag, and *nothing* publishes a domain-placed label or entity. Nobody could list the writers, and the shipped behavior no longer matched the philosophy.
3. It was **never exposed in the UI**, so users could not operate it — only code did, inconsistently. (Its near-zero deliberate usage in the fleet is therefore *not* evidence about demand, and is not used as an argument here.)
4. Some access paths ignored it entirely: the actor pickers and the users list gave different answers about the same person, and the document-template picker replaced the flag with a hardcoded `builtin` literal (§7.4).

**This specification introduces no new authorization decision primitive: ambient visibility becomes ordinary RBAC data — derived member groups plus removable role assignments, surfaced as a single folder control. The resolver contains zero visibility-specific rules, and RoleAssignment gains no lifecycle state. In one line: principal group + semantically-defined role + folder scope.**

## 2. Foundations — facts about `main` today

This section states only what is currently true; the constraints this specification *adds* live in §4 and §6.

1. **The RBAC data model.** Folders form the organization tree. A Role is a set of per-model CRUD permissions. A RoleAssignment binds a principal to a role over perimeter folders, optionally recursive. The schema supports **both** user-group principals and direct-user principals, and `RoleAssignmentWriteSerializer` exposes all fields — nothing today prevents creating a human direct-user assignment. (The two-kinds principal rule, its write-time validation, and the migration precondition are **new requirements** — §4.3, §6.)
2. **The single decision primitive.** Every access question is answered by `is_access_allowed(permission, folder)`, or by its bulk form. A permission check always names a folder; instance-wide operations use the root folder.
3. **Enforcement points.** Lists are filtered in `get_queryset`, creation is checked in serializers, single-object access in `RBACPermissions`.
4. **Managed IAM groups.** The platform generates per-folder role×domain groups from available roles, governed by the `create_iam_groups` toggle; IdP-synchronized groups map onto them; no built-in role carries `add_usergroup`, so tenants cannot create additional principal groups. Users are created in the root folder; a scheduled task deactivates users past their `expiry_date` (expiry manifests as `is_active=False`). The `descendants` closure table is maintained on folder changes.

## 3. The mental model (documentation only — never code vocabulary)

The org tree is a **building**: Global is the building, domains are floors, sub-domains are rooms. Role assignments are **badges**; a role is a **badge profile** — which drawer types (models) a badge opens, with which gestures (view / add / change / delete). Content lives in typed **drawers**.

A floor's **members list** — everyone working on that floor or below — exists exactly while the floor gives its members a **default profile**: hand the members a default profile and the list comes into being; withdraw it and the list goes with it. Nothing else ever references the list, so removing it breaks nothing else.

Every building ships with one decision already made: the ground floor's list is on, and its members carry the `Reader Catalog` profile — **the library, the vocabulary and the people directory are open to everyone who works here**. A renovated building keeps, for a while, the wider profile it had before the works (`Reader Migration (legacy)`), until its manager reads what changed and chooses.

That default is a **dial, not a law**: any floor's default profile can be changed, up to full `Reader` (radical transparency) or down to a single drawer type, or to nothing at all. Nothing else is automatic. **Visitors** (third parties) are on no members list, ever. **Street-entrance meeting rooms** (enclaves) don't put anyone on a floor's list and keep no list of their own. Sharing is placement plus badges — putting content where its readers already reach, or handing someone a badge to the room that holds it — never a property of the object, and never a side effect of delivering content.

| Analogy | Real term |
|---|---|
| Building / floor / room | Global folder / domain / sub-domain |
| Badge, badge profile | RoleAssignment, Role |
| Badge touch on a drawer | `is_access_allowed(perm, folder)` |
| Floor members list | `<domain> - members` managed group |
| Default profile for a floor's members | The folder's "default role" control (projection of one assignment row; setting it creates the list, clearing it dissolves it) |
| Visitor | Third-party principal |
| Street-entrance meeting room | Enclave folder |

The analogy is explanatory only. Code and API keep the real names.

## 4. The model (normative)

There is **no ambient-visibility mechanism**. Visibility is `is_access_allowed(view_M, folder)` — nothing else, for anyone, ever. One companion fact is recognized rather than invented: **nested serialization is a visibility path** — content may ride an aggregate the caller can read (embedded components and related names), governed by the host object's check, in the spirit of components-follow-roots. Conformance row 1 therefore claims uniqueness of *folder-visibility decision paths*, not of every byte's route to a screen.

What used to be "publication" is expressed entirely as data:

### 4.1 The member groups

**Lifecycle — bound to the default role.** Nothing is stored on Folder: no visibility semantics, and no provisioning state either — the member group's on/off state *is* the presence of the folder's default role. Setting the default role (§4.2) creates `<F> - members`, carrying an **immutable structural discriminator**: a protected managed-group kind (`DOMAIN_MEMBERS`) set at creation and never editable. The engine keys its special behavior on this kind, never on names or codename prefixes. Lifecycle and folder binding are protected: `DOMAIN_MEMBERS` groups are created and deleted **only** by default-role transitions, and their folder is never reassignable. **The group is referenced by exactly one row — its own folder's default role** (write-time validation rejects any other assignment naming a `DOMAIN_MEMBERS` principal), so clearing the default role deletes the group as a fully local act: no cross-folder effect exists, nothing can dangle, and ordinary role-assignment audit logging covers the transitions. **Enclave folders may not have a default role** (write-time rejected) — enclaves receive explicit grants only; a member audience for a visitor space contradicts its purpose. **Invariant, checked both ways: the member group exists iff the folder has a default role; no other reference to it can exist.**

**Derivation (explicit source set).** **Effective membership of a UserGroup** is one defined relation: direct membership ∪ (when the `idp_groups` feature is enabled) membership via a mapped IdP group — consumed by **both** principal resolution and the member-group evaluator, so they can never disagree. `<F> - members` = the **active** internal human users with effective membership in the **role×domain generated groups** of `F` or of any descendant of `F` reachable without crossing an enclave boundary: **enclave exclusion is positional, by path** — a generated group contributes members only if the path from its folder up to the audience folder crosses no enclave boundary (sufficient even when ordinary folders exist beneath an enclave; nested configurations are tested). Excluded from the source set: derived member groups themselves (principals, never sources), third-party principals (`User.is_third_party`), service accounts, and deactivated accounts (`is_active=False`; account expiry manifests as deactivation via the scheduled task, so it takes effect within the task's cadence). **A member group may legitimately have zero effective members.**

**Evaluation — virtual, in the IAM engine (no materialization, no synchronization).** The member group is a real `UserGroup` row (assignment principal, picker entry, register identity) that stores **no membership**. Membership is computed at evaluation time inside principal resolution, with one closure join: *user's effective generated groups → their folders → non-enclave-crossing ancestors via the `descendants` closure → the member groups existing on those ancestors* — exclusions as query filters. No independently materialized member-group state can become stale; evaluation reflects the current committed source memberships and folder closure, so changes take effect on the first evaluation after the source transaction commits. One canonical helper (`is_member(user, folder)` / `members_of(folder)`) serves the engine, the UI's live member list, and any future feature — **and it is the only door**: no authorization path may join the raw group-membership table for member groups; interactive checks, bulk folder resolution, RAG filtering, exports, and background jobs all use this abstraction. This is one **derived-principal expansion rule in group resolution** — principal semantics, not visibility semantics; the visibility layer keeps zero special rules.

**Performance budget (normative).** The expansion adds **at most one additional indexed query per principal resolution**, with the index plan covering the group kind and the closure through-table. `members_of(Global)` is an administrative UI operation: paginated, with an expected cost of one users×groups×closure sweep — acceptable for a page, never on a hot path. **Request-scoped memoization is permitted** (one resolution per request); the caching prohibition concerns **cross-request** state: the initial implementation does not cache expanded membership across requests, and later caching requires documented revocation consistency, invalidation rules, and failure behavior.

**Integrity.** With no materialized state, there is nothing to synchronize, reconcile, or quarantine. What remains: a **property test** in CI asserting the virtual expansion ≡ an independent reference recompute; correctness tests for enclave and nested-enclave configurations, deactivation, and multi-path membership.

**Correctness rests on the folder closure**, which `descendants` materializes and every recursive role assignment already depends on today — so `manage.py check_iam_closure` is not a dependency this specification introduces but **platform hygiene that pays for itself before member groups exist**, and it ships in release 1 (§8).

- **Prevention first.** A static CI check that no code writes `parent_folder` in bulk (`QuerySet.update`) is the primary defence; the runtime check catches what slips past it.
- **When it runs.** After every migration, reusing the wiring `startup()` already has — but note what that wiring actually is: `post_migrate` is emitted **once per installed app with a models module** (and again after `flush`), so an unfiltered receiver runs repeatedly. `startup()` copes by self-filtering to the last migratable app (`core/startup.py`); the closure check must do the same, or carry an explicit sender filter plus an idempotency guard, or be invoked once from the deployment workflow. It also runs **at the end of a restore** — the other realistic way a stale closure arrives, and the case `ready()`'s `RUN_MAIN` guard explicitly steps around — and **on demand from the audit page**, because these hooks catch deployment-time drift only: corruption introduced by running application code waits for the next deploy unless someone can ask.

- **Ordering, since the closure defines the baseline the report is computed against.** Both report generation and release 2 follow one sequence: **(1)** validate and repair the closure; **(2)** if the repair changed anything, bump the **`closure_epoch`**, which is an input to the analysis fingerprint (§6.4) — so every report drawn against the old topology is stale by construction; **(3)** generate or validate the report; **(4)** evaluate acknowledgements and the other gates; **(5)** apply the migration. §11.2 lists the same order.
- **What it does: detect, repair, record — in that order.** The closure holds no information the parent pointers do not already imply, so recomputation is always safe and idempotent; leaving a corrupt authorization structure in place while waiting for a human is the worse trade, since the over-grant direction is a live hole. Repair runs in a transaction under `Folder._lock_folder_tree()` — the primitive `Folder.save` already uses — so two concurrent runs (a rollout that migrates per pod rather than once) cannot interleave.
- **Recording is not optional, and includes direction.** A repair is an access change: counts, affected folder ids, and **whether effective access narrowed or widened**, because narrowing is what generates "I could see this yesterday". Findings surface on the IAM audit page, not only in logs.
- **Failure behaviour** follows the general rule: alarm and repair, never refuse to boot. At `post_migrate` that is not merely consistency — refusing would fail deployments, which is worse than the condition it would be protesting.

### 4.2 The folder control and its assignment

The control is a **projection, never a column**: what is stored is the assignment row itself; the folder form reads and writes it, and the role-assignments register shows the same row. One source of truth, two views. It requires **IAM-administration rights** on the folder (the permission that governs role assignments there, not mere `change_folder`). It is a new IAM UI surface.

**The control.** `<F> - members` holds exactly one role, on `F` alone: one nullable value, at most one row per folder (`<F> - members × role × {F}`). Setting it creates the member group; clearing it deletes the group (§4.1). *Empty means no ambient visibility here; non-empty means this is what members read here.* Composition is choosing a role that already contains what is wanted, never stacking rows — the standard posture below answers almost every folder, and a custom role covers the rest. A member group can be granted nothing anywhere but on its own folder; there is no cross-folder audience control.

**Role eligibility.** Because the audience is derived and large, a role used as a default role must contain **only `view_*` permissions** and **no `Plumbing` permissions** (§4.5). Write and administrative capability reaches people through explicit assignments to generated groups, never through an ambient audience.

**Where eligibility is enforced.** When the default role is set, and again **whenever a role already in use as a default is edited** — otherwise a later edit to a custom role silently hands a whole branch the ability to change or delete. Both sides live in many-to-many relations, so `Role.save()` and `RoleAssignment.save()` are insufficient hooks. Coverage must include `permissions.add/remove/set/clear`, `perimeter_folders.add/remove/set/clear`, bulk and through-table writes, and deletion through the generic register. The implementation routes these through **canonical mutation services** with `m2m_changed` **backstops**, and a static check prohibits bulk or through-table writes outside those services — the same pattern as the `parent_folder` rule.

**Row shape.** The assignment is an ordinary, admin-owned, removable row — not builtin-protected, never re-created behind an admin's back — constrained to: exactly one perimeter folder, which is the group's own; `is_recursive = False`; at most one default-role row per folder.

**Why non-recursion is load-bearing.** A recursive default role would expose descendant-folder content within the branch — a sensitive library loaded into a sub-folder would become readable by the whole branch — defeating placement-scoping.

**How the shape is enforced.** These conditions span `RoleAssignment`, its many-to-many perimeter, and the principal group's kind and folder binding, so a plain database `CHECK` cannot express them. Enforcement is **transactional domain validation**: every transition runs in one transaction under `Folder._lock_folder_tree()`, backed by a **standing integrity check** in the audit tool that reports any row violating the shape. A relational shape carrying the constraint natively, or deferred constraint triggers, would be stronger; both remain open to implementation and neither is assumed.

**The lock.** `SELECT … FOR UPDATE` on the **root folder row** — a *global* folder-tree lock, not a subtree lock — and the `FOR UPDATE` is **PostgreSQL-only**; on SQLite it degrades to `atomic()`, which the codebase considers sufficient there. Two consequences: it is acceptable only because these are rare administrative writes, and **concurrency tests must run on PostgreSQL**, where the behaviour exists.

**A fresh install ships one decision, one row**: `Global - members × Reader Catalog × {Global}` — Global's member group comes into existence with it. A **migrated tenant** gets `Reader Migration (legacy)` instead, on Global and on every eligible folder (§6.2) — same shape, at most one row per folder.

**The three built-in roles** (tenant-immutable, product-managed):

| Role | Contents | Generates groups? | Used as |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reader Catalog** (`BI-RL-CAT`) | Every model classified **Catalog** — the reference material an organization shares with itself: frameworks and their requirement nodes, questions and choices, matrices, threats, reference controls, metric definitions, terminologies, labels, asset classes, classification schemes and levels, custom-field definitions, mapping sets, TTP catalogues, document templates, stored and loaded libraries — plus the **directory** (`User`, with the safe projection of §4.2) and `Folder`, without which breadcrumbs, pickers and folder trees break. | **No** | The fresh-install default at Global, and the recommended posture anywhere |
| **Reader Migration (legacy)** (`BI-RL-MIG`) | **Frozen**: exactly the models `is_published` published *in practice* on `main` — Catalog, plus the operational family (assets, applied controls and policies, evidence, vulnerabilities, security exceptions, incidents, timeline entries, comments), the root-mixin definitions and records (entities, metric instances, organisation issues and objectives, risk acceptances, responsibility roles), the directory (users, teams, actors), the document tree, and `DocumentTemplate`, which release 1 publishes by default (§7.4). It is a **compatibility artifact**, not a design: **no new model ever joins it**, and a tenant is expected to move to `Reader Catalog` or `Reader` once it has read its diff report. | **No** | Assigned by release 2's migration; selectable afterwards |
| **Reader** (`BI-RL-AUD`) | Every model classified **Catalog or Content** — everything a user may read — and **no `Plumbing`** (§4.5). | **Yes** (existing built-in group) | Radical transparency, and the ordinary reader role for people |

**A role carries a flag saying whether it is materialised as per-folder groups.** Today `Folder.create_default_ug_and_ra` walks a hardcoded list of six `(group, role)` pairs (`iam/models.py`); that list becomes a **declarative boolean on `Role`**, so the catalogue cannot silently disagree with what gets provisioned. `Reader Catalog` and `Reader Migration (legacy)` carry it **false**: they are postures for member groups, not profiles people are placed into, and materialising them would add groups and assignments to every domain for no one's benefit. The flag is **product-managed and never tenant-settable to true** — flipping it on a custom role would provision a group *and* an assignment in every folder with `create_iam_groups`, a provisioning explosion and a privilege surface. A role with the flag false is still fully eligible as a default role; the two questions are independent:

- *Is this role materialised as per-folder groups?* — the new flag.
- *May this role be a default role?* — the eligibility rule above (`view_*` only, no `Plumbing`).

**Built-in roles are semantically defined.** `Reader Catalog` *means* "may read the organization's reference material"; its permission list is the current answer to that semantic, not its identity. It evolves under three rules:

- **(0) One-time classification.** Every model that exists today must be classified once — **Catalog**, **Content**, or **Plumbing**-with-a-named-class. Roughly 180 models, a one-time exercise rather than a by-product of CI, and the roles are *derived* from its output: `Reader Catalog` is Catalog, `Reader` is Catalog ∪ Content, `Plumbing` is in neither. §4.5's inventory seeds the Plumbing side and `Reader`'s current contents the rest, every disagreement resolved deliberately (that is how the §7.3 gaps were found). It is a **release 1 deliverable**: without the manifest there are no derived roles and no migration predicate. Note what this question *is not*: it is "is this reference material?", which is nearly always obvious, rather than "is this assessment work?", which is contested at the boundary and was the reason an "everything except assessments" posture was rejected as unusable.
- **(i) New models.** A new model is classified in the same release that introduces it — a CI rule fails the build on omission, and a `Plumbing` answer must name its class. A model classified Catalog joins `Reader Catalog`, so existing assignments **prospectively authorize all future rows** of it; that follows the semantic contract and is stated so nobody meets it by surprise. **`Reader Migration (legacy)` never grows**: it is frozen at what `main` published, and a new model joining it would be meaningless.
- **(ii) Reclassification.** Moving an *existing* model between classes is an **access migration** — measured, reviewed and release-noted with the same discipline as the migration itself.

There is no role versioning: one role, one meaning, fleet-wide. **Custom copies do not follow**: a custom role copied from a built-in is a static permission set, so upgrade reporting identifies custom roles that shadow a built-in's contents and lets administrators decide whether to extend them. Tenants never edit the built-in roles; customization means replacing an assignment's role with a custom copy.

**Directory reads are safe for everyone, by design.** All normal User reads expose the **safe projection** (picker fields) regardless of which role granted them; administrative metadata — MFA, IdP state, admin flags — is served exclusively by a separate administrative endpoint under its own permission. No serializer conditionality anywhere: role provenance is unknowable in the primitive, so the projection cannot depend on it.

**Teams and entities are standard objects** — like applied controls: visible where roles reach, shareable by placement and ordinary grants, never ambient by birthright. (They are in `Reader Migration (legacy)` because `main` published them; they are not in `Reader Catalog`.) A shared vendor registry, where an organization wants one, is the shelf pattern (§5.4). The Actor delegate needs no change: `readable(Actor)` = readable(User) ∪ readable(Team) ∪ readable(Entity).

### 4.3 Invariants (each is a conformance test)

1. **No per-object visibility state exists anywhere** — no flag, no column, no property.
2. **No visibility-specific code exists in the resolver.** `readable(u, M)` ≡ `get_allowed_folder_ids(u, view_M)`; the target plan's overlay seam closes empty.
3. **RoleAssignment has no lifecycle state.** No enabled/status field, no provenance field — every row is an ordinary grant, including the ones the migration creates.
4. **Human role-assignment principals are exactly two kinds** — generated role×domain groups, and derived member groups. **Write-time validation is the primary enforcement**: serializer/model-level rejection of human direct-user principals, of kind-discriminator edits, of member-group lifecycle operations outside the default-role transitions, and of any assignment naming a `DOMAIN_MEMBERS` principal on any folder but its own. Boot checks are a **detection backstop** whose failure behavior is start + alarm + repair guidance — data findings never refuse boot.
5. **Member groups are derived-only** (no UI/API mutates membership); existence obeys the default-role binding (§4.1) — the group exists iff its folder has a default role — with no other references possible.
6. **Identity gates ambience**: third-party principals, service accounts, and deactivated accounts never contribute to member groups; enclave exclusion is **positional, by path** (§4.1) — a property of grants, not an identity class. A service account's access is **entirely explicit and fine-grained**: the creation flow already lets an administrator select the exact permissions the integration needs, which become a per-account anonymous role (or, alternatively, one built-in role), assigned over chosen perimeter folders via the machine-reserved direct path. No built-in reader role exists for machines and none is needed — an integration that reads frameworks and resolves owners gets `view_framework` and `view_user`, not a class-wide grant, which is stricter than any role the product could ship for it. Nothing about a service account is ambient: whatever it must read at the root folder must appear in its own assignment.
7. **Content flows never create or modify IAM.** Loading a library or authoring definitions places content only (a passive informational note is permitted). Lifecycle features (publishing a document, issuing a finding) create an **independent, immutable published aggregate** (snapshot + manifest) and **place** it where its audience already reads — never grants. **This is target behaviour owned by the findings/documents roadmap, and neither release of this project builds it**; what is normative here is only the prohibition: no lifecycle feature may write IAM. **Until snapshot publication ships, the supported workflow is placement plus grants**: the container stays where it is, and downward sharing is that folder's default role or a direct grant on the folder holding it (§5.4). Any future workflow that must create an assignment requires the IAM-administration permission explicitly.
8. **Components follow their aggregate root; delegates follow their delegatees** (Actor ≡ readable User ∪ Team ∪ Entity — §7.1). Nothing that is a component ever changes folder on its own.
9. **Role permission lists are the taxonomy.** Built-in role semantics are fleet-consistent; their permission lists are product-managed and may change only under the role-evolution rules (§4.2).

### 4.4 Deliberately absent

These are boundaries of the design, not open questions:

- **Per-object visibility state** of any kind — no flag, column, or property. Evidence-gated reintroduction on a single model remains possible, but nothing in §5 needs it.
- **Per-model conditioning in the resolver** (main's "amplifier", which filtered ambient visibility by the caller's own permissions). Its least-privilege function is expressible as policy: choose a narrower default role.
- **Cross-folder audiences** — a member group cannot be granted on another folder. Sub-folder-granularity sharing is a maintained list (§5.4), not a live audience. Restoring live cross-folder audiences requires a new ADR, since it also requires an existence rule independent of the folder's own default role.
- **Materialized membership**, and with it synchronization, reconciliation, health flags, and quarantine.
- **Role versioning.** Built-in roles are semantic and evolve under §4.2's two rules.
- **Role-provenance-dependent serializers** ("safe user fields only if access came via some directory role") — unimplementable in a primitive that returns booleans; superseded by the universal safe User projection (§4.2).
- **A recursive exception for the directory**, and recursive default roles generally: non-recursion is a blanket rule (§4.2).
- **An "all active internal accounts" principal.** A user in no managed group sees nothing, by design.
- **Reproducing main's drifted behavior** for anyone — no compatibility assignments, no per-principal reach measurement feeding grants. Integrations are kept working by an administrator adjusting their explicit assignments (§6.6), not by replaying what they happened to see.
- **Reader roles for machines.** A service account gets a per-account permission set, never a shipped posture. The three built-in roles are for folders, not integrations, and narrowing below `Reader Catalog` is a custom role.

### 4.5 What no ambient posture carries — the plumbing inventory (reviewer-auditable)

Because both built-in roles are derived from the classification, `Reader` contains every model classified Assessment or Content, and **Plumbing** is what neither posture ever carries. That claim is easy to assert and tedious to check, so here is the complete inventory as of this writing — **32 models absent from `Reader` today** — with the role that *does* hold each one, because "plumbing" means *never ambient*, not *invisible to everyone*: an administrator or an analyst may legitimately read some of these in their own scope.

**Tier 1 — no read permission in any role (17).** Internal rows with no standalone endpoint, read through their aggregate root (invariant 8) or not at all: `CustomFieldChoice`, `CustomFieldValue`, `RequirementNodeAttachment`, `DocumentReference`, `HistoricalMetric`, `BuiltinMetricSample`, `FlowEvent`, `RequirementAssignmentEvent`, `SyncEvent`, `PostureRun`, `ConditionBranch`, `IntegrationProvider`, `IntegrationSchemaCache`, `RoleAssignment`, `ServiceAccount`, `PersonalAccessToken`, `SCIMToken`. Custom-field rendering is served by the form-schema projection (§5.8), not by reading these.

**Tier 2 — administrator surface (10).** Readable by `ADMINISTRATOR` only, and rightly so — the access map, credential-bearing configuration, authoring and rendering machinery: `Role`, `IdPGroup`, `SSOSettings`, `WebhookEndpoint`, `WebhookEventType`, `LibraryDraft`, `CustomWordTemplate`, `CustomDocHtmlTemplate`, `CustomEmailTemplate`. `cal.Event` sits here by accident: it has no folder, no RBAC integration and no consumer — a removal candidate, not an exclusion decision.

**Tier 3 — held by lower roles but missing from `Reader` (5).** These are the interesting ones, because `Reader`'s rule is *all view permissions*, so anything an Analyst or Domain Manager may read and a Reader may not is a gap unless a Plumbing class justifies it:

| Model | Held by | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| `OrganisationIssue`, `OrganisationObjective` | DM, Admin | **Bug** — organisational context, no reason to withhold. Fixed in §7.3; they become Content and join the posture. |
| `LibraryFilteringLabel` | Analyst, DM, Admin | **Bug** — its sibling `FilteringLabel` is in `Reader`, it has its own endpoint, and it is a label catalogue. Fixed in §7.3; becomes Content. |
| `DocumentEdit` | Analyst, DM, Admin | **Plumbing** (component of a revision, no standalone endpoint). The Analyst grant is inert. |
| `IntegrationConfiguration` | Analyst, DM, Admin | **Plumbing** (credential-bearing connection config). Kept out of `Reader` deliberately: the serializer masks secrets today, but a permission should not be the last line between a reader and a credential. Its Analyst-level grant is a **pre-existing question for a separate review**, not something this specification widens or narrows. |

The two Plumbing classes are therefore: **internal rows** (tier 1, plus `DocumentEdit`) and the **administrator surface** — access map, credentials and configuration, authoring machinery (tier 2). Nothing else may be classified Plumbing without naming one of them. Not part of the inventory at all: enum classes (`Severity`, `FieldType`) and Django manager/queryset classes, which have no permissions to grant; and `GlobalSettings` itself — its own permission (`view_globalsettings`) is §7.2's subject, not a classification question. Its child models are a different matter: `SSOSettings(GlobalSettings)`, the only one today, carries its own `view_ssosettings` permission and is an ordinary administrator-surface row, listed in tier 2 above.

## 5. Sharing patterns (how every use case is expressed)

1. **Org-wide catalog and directory**: ships working — Global's default role (`Reader Catalog`). A migrated tenant starts wider, on `Reader Migration (legacy)`.
2. **Sensitive/domain catalog** *(follow-up feature — today `LibraryImporter` loads into Global only)*: load the library into the domain, then set the domain's default role (the member group comes into existence with it). The load itself never grants anything. The pattern applies today to domain-authored definitions (labels, asset classes, classification schemes, custom fields).
3. **Narrower domain postures**: a custom role as the folder's default role where `Reader Catalog` is still too wide (§4.2).
4. **The shelf pattern** (per-object sharing at folder granularity): create a sub-folder (`EMEA/Shared`), move the objects in, then grant the shelf to its readers directly — put them in the shelf's own generated group(s). Explicit and auditable; a maintained list, not a live audience (live audiences exist at folder granularity, through default roles). A shelf never inherits its parent's default; it must be granted deliberately. The same pattern serves a shared **vendor registry** (entities) or shared team rosters.
5. **Publishing a document / issuing a finding** *(target behaviour — not built by either release; see §4.3-7)*: the lifecycle act creates an independent, immutable published aggregate and **places** it where its audience already reads (a shelf, the enclave, the portal). Placement, not grants. **Interim, and today:** the document keeps its own folder, and its readers are covered by that folder's default role or a direct grant — publication remains a lifecycle state, with no IAM effect of its own.
6. **"Publish a risk analysis as a template"**: derive a frozen definition-kind copy (snapshot / library-builder path) and place it where its readers are — as a definition rather than an assessment, it falls inside the standard posture.
7. **Audience-scoped sharing**: place the content in the folder whose default role already covers that audience — or, below folder granularity, grant the holding sub-folder to the audience's people directly (§5.4).
8. **Custom-field definitions and form rendering**: rendering-critical schema is UI configuration, not data. Form rendering consumes a **restricted form-schema projection** (name, type, choices) available to anyone who can view or edit the host object — independent of any grant on `CustomFieldDefinition` itself — so configured fields are **never silently absent** from forms. Full `CustomFieldDefinition` objects remain RBAC'd like everything else.

## 6. Migration & transition

**The target is the original intent, not the measured drift.** What `is_published` was built to mean — *everything is shared with the organization except assessments* — is the owner-approved posture, while its encoding never matched it (§1.2) and the resolver additionally conditioned everything per user (only holders of `view_M` below ever saw a published object). Reproducing that accident exactly has little value: it is not what anyone designed or documented. The migration therefore **restores the intent and discloses the difference**.

1. **Two releases.** **Release 1** ships the diff report (as the IAM audit tool and its headless command, §11), the migration-exclusion record (item 3), the direct-user validator, and the §7 fixes. It creates **no default role and no member group** — no posture migration occurs while `is_published` still runs. It is not access-neutral, and does not claim to be: the §7 fixes deliberately change access (actor pickers resolve, `view_globalsettings` leaves the non-admin roles, three Reader gaps close), each small, each release-noted. **Release 2** applies the posture and drops the columns, atomically.
2. **One posture, applied where it has an effect.** Release 2's migration sets **`Reader Migration (legacy)`** as the default role of exactly this set: **Global — always, and not excludable — plus every folder that is not an enclave, holds at least one object of a model in the legacy role's list, and carries no migration exclusion (item 4)** — one row per folder, the member group coming into existence with it. The three qualifiers each matter: enclaves reject default roles by §4.1, `Plumbing` objects are non-assessment but must never cause a default role, and an excluded folder is one an operator deliberately kept out. Folders outside the set get nothing. The content test is a **one-time snapshot**, deliberately: a folder that receives its first content after the migration keeps no default role until an administrator sets one, and **folders created after release 2 start with none** — the same state a fresh install's non-Global folders have. Global always gets the row, so every tenant has the fleet-wide posture at the top whatever happens below.
3. **The old-reach simulator, and its fidelity requirement.** Everything below rests on one component: an evaluator that answers *what does `main` expose today* for a given principal and model. It is a **second implementation of an authorization rule** — the published branch with its per-model conditioning, the root mixin, policy-backed document inheritance and enclave exclusion — and §1.4 is the standing reminder of what two implementations of one truth do when they drift. It therefore carries a **differential fidelity test**: for a sampled cross-product of principals and models on a real dataset, the simulator's answer must equal what the live `main` code path returns, and the test runs while `main`'s code still exists — which is to say, in release 1, because release 2 deletes the thing it compares against. A simulator that is subtly wrong does not merely mis-report: the service-account gate and the direct-user precondition would then gate on fiction.

4. **The diff report is disclosure, not an approval gate — and it carries the three artifacts release 2 consumes** (release 1, per tenant, exhaustive while the flags still exist).

   **Migration exclusions.** "Pre-clearing a folder" cannot mean clearing a default role, because none exists until release 2. It is instead a **durable exclusion record kept outside IAM** — a small migration-scoped table written through the audit tool, listing folder ids with who excluded them and why. Release 2's migration reads it as part of the §6.2 predicate and records the outcome; it grants nothing and expires with the migration.

   **A freshness fingerprint — over the report's findings, not the tenant's rows.** A report generated months before the upgrade may describe a different tenant, but hashing every securable object's `(id, model, folder, is_published)` would be both expensive (a multi-million-row scan on a large tenant, repeated at every validation) and *over-sensitive*: editing one unrelated `Answer` would retire conclusions it cannot affect. The fingerprint therefore covers **what the report asserts**, produced by the same pass that writes it:

   - the per-folder posture decisions (which folders qualify, which do not, and why);
   - the per-principal deltas and the drill-down object lists behind them;
   - the per-account service-account loss sets;
   - the migration exclusions, the `closure_epoch` (§4.1), the classification manifest, and the report-algorithm version.

   The property is preserved and sharpened: **anything that would change an assertion changes the hash, and nothing else does.** Validating a report means re-running the analysis pass — the same work as generating one, which is why the cheap operational answer is simply to regenerate.

   **Service-account acknowledgements match on the loss set, not on the whole report.** An operator reasoned about one machine's losses, so an acknowledgement — `(service_account, loss_set_hash, algorithm_version, disposition, actor, timestamp)` — is honoured while that account's recomputed loss set still hashes the same; it does not retire because an unrelated folder gained a document. The report fingerprint gates *the report*; the loss-set hash gates *each decision*. Acknowledgements remain outside both hashes, so neither is self-referential.

   **In the destructive transaction, the check is a watermark, not a rescan.** The application is down or read-only for that window, so nothing can be writing; re-running the analysis there — the most expensive possible moment, with the tenant offline — would only re-prove what the window guarantees. The transaction instead verifies a **cheap change detector** captured when the gates passed: per-relevant-table row counts, `max(updated_at)` watermarks, and the `closure_epoch`. A mismatch means the window was not honoured, and the migration aborts.

   The report itself covers, per folder and **per model family**, which principals gain what, and what goes dark. **What was ambient on `main` depends on placement, not only on family, and the report says so**: the twenty-one `PublishInRootFolderMixin` models — twenty-two with the `Policy` proxy — were ambient **at the root folder only** (labels, terminologies, asset classes, classification schemes, custom-field definitions, responsibility roles, entities, metric instances, organisation issues and objectives, risk acceptances, and the threat-intelligence referentials `Threat`/`CWE`/`Technique`/`SecurityAdvisory`), so applying the posture to a *domain* is genuinely new access for them; documents were ambient **only when policy-backed**, so standalone documents are new access wherever the posture lands (document templates are excluded from this disclosure: release 1 publishes them, so they are already ambient when release 2 runs — §7.4); the `default=True` operational family was ambient everywhere and changes nothing. **Newly ambient placements are drillable, not just counted**: for each one the report gives the object count and the identifiers and names behind it, because "37 documents at Global become readable org-wide" is not actionable and "these 37, by name" is — that is how a tenant finds the three confidential contracts that need moving before the upgrade. The upgrade does **not** block on a per-tenant decision: the report and the release notes carry the change, tightening afterwards is one value in one dropdown, and a folder that should stay out entirely goes on the exclusion list. **Global is the one folder that cannot be excluded** — it carries the fleet-wide posture, and allowing an exception there would mean migrated tenants no longer converge on it. A tenant with confidential objects at the root folder moves them before the upgrade (the drill-down names them, one by one) — but **moving is re-audiencing, not hiding**: a domain folder that receives a `Content` object thereby qualifies for the posture, and the object becomes ambient to *that* domain's members. The remedy is to move it **to a folder whose member audience is the intended one** — typically a leaf folder whose branch is exactly the people who should read it — **or to a folder carrying a migration exclusion**. Placement is the remedy, not an exemption, and placement means choosing an audience.
5. **Tightening is the fallback.** An administrator who does not want the posture at a folder clears its default role: members then see exactly what their own roles grant, and sharing is done by placement and explicit grants (§5). There is **no compatibility-assignment generator for human principals**.
6. **Service accounts are reported, not migrated.** The loss gate below makes this safe whatever the installed base: the report lists every account with its permissions and perimeter folders **and computes what each one loses** — the models it reads today through the published branch and will not read afterwards — so an administrator can extend the assignment before an integration breaks (typically adding the root folder to its perimeter). Machines are the class that fails silently at 03:00, so they get the same loss analysis humans get — and, unlike humans, a **gate**: release 2 refuses to run unless either **no service-account losses are reported**, or **each affected account is explicitly acknowledged** in the report as remediated or knowingly accepted. What they still do not get is grants: the migration creates none for them — no compatibility assignments, no reach reproduction.
7. **Reported gains and losses — much smaller now that the migration assigns a legacy-shaped role.** Because `Reader Migration (legacy)` is built from the practice map (§1.2), the families `main` published stay published: the operational family, the root-mixin definitions and records — including `RiskAcceptance`, whose root-placed rows the mixin publishes automatically — the directory, and the document tree. What the report must still disclose falls into three kinds. **Model-level approximation**: a role grants per model, while `main` published per object, so *standalone* documents become ambient where only policy-backed ones were, and domain-placed labels or entities become ambient where only root-placed ones were. **The amplifier's removal**: `main` filtered ambient visibility by the caller's own permissions, and a role does not — so a narrow custom role gains everything the legacy role lists, which is the largest remaining widening and is disclosed per principal. **Hand-set flags**: objects a raw-API caller unpublished become visible again (the PR #4578 backfill is the precedent), and objects it published on models outside the legacy list go dark — the fleet scan shows artifact-dominated singles in both directions. Conversely, hand-hidden operational objects become visible wherever the posture applies (the precedent is the PR #4578 backfill). Third-party respondents are a **verified non-dependency**: their enclave assignment is excluded by both of main's published branches (an intentional, tested invariant), and questionnaire content reaches them through aggregate-scoped nested serialization.
8. **Prospective effects, stated openly.** Members joining later get the posture rather than main's per-role filtering; new models join the posture by classification (§4.2). Both are the design working as intended, permanently.
9. **Execution boundary — the migration is a two-run protocol, deliberately.** The order in §4.1 has a trap if everything runs in one atomic block: a closure repair bumps `closure_epoch`, the report's analysis fingerprint stops matching, the run halts — and the rollback undoes the repair and the epoch, so the next run repeats the cycle forever. The boundary is therefore explicit:

   It is **two migration files**, not one non-atomic migration with a hand-rolled inner transaction — in an `atomic = False` migration, ordinary `RemoveField` operations are not enclosed by a preceding Python `atomic()` block, and forcing that would need a custom schema operation:

   - **Preflight migration (`atomic = False`).** Validates and repairs the closure in a committed transaction. If the repair changed anything, it bumps `closure_epoch` and **aborts with instructions to regenerate the report** — the epoch survives, because it was committed rather than rolled back. On the next run it passes without changes and is recorded as applied. It is a one-time gate for this upgrade; ongoing closure protection remains the `post_migrate`, post-restore and run-now invocations of §4.1.
   - **Destructive migration (`atomic = True`).** With the application down or read-only, and in this order at the start of the transaction: **a detect-only closure check first** — the preflight is recorded as applied, so a retry days later has nothing else re-checking the closure, and corruption can appear without `closure_epoch` moving; if it is corrupt the migration **aborts with instructions to run the repair command and regenerate the report**, and it **never repairs inside this transaction** (a repair here would be rolled back by any later abort, resurrecting the cycle the preflight exists to break). Then validate the report's findings fingerprint and evaluate every gate, verify the change detector captured when they passed (row counts, watermarks, `closure_epoch` — not a rescan, §6.4), create the roles, member groups and default-role assignments, and drop the 158 `is_published` columns — committing all of it together, or nothing.
   - **What "atomic" covers, precisely.** The transaction covers **database changes only**: the assignments created and the columns dropped. **Removing the legacy resolver branches and the per-model `default=True` writers is a code change**, shipped by the same maintenance deployment and not transactional with the DDL — which is exactly what rollback documentation must say, since reverting the code and reverting the schema are two different acts. `SCHEMA_VERSION = 3` likewise ships as a code constant in that release, not as a migration operation.

   A gate failure inside the destructive migration rolls back cleanly and leaves the migration unrecorded: the operator fixes the finding and re-runs, while the preflight's committed work stands. That is what breaks the repair-invalidate-abort cycle.

   **The check-to-use race is closed by the upgrade window, not by hope.** Validation and application are separated in time, so the inputs could change between them. The destructive migration therefore requires the application to be **down or read-only for its duration** — the ordinary upgrade posture for a schema-changing release — or, where that is impossible, a migration-wide lock honoured by every mutation path that feeds the fingerprint. As a last check inside that window, the migration **verifies inside the final atomic block the change detector captured when the gates passed** (row counts, `max(updated_at)` watermarks, `closure_epoch` — §6.4), and aborts on mismatch; aborting there is safe and does not loop, since the repair and epoch from the earlier phase are already committed.

10. **Preconditions that halt the destructive migration**: **human direct-user role assignments** (IAM hygiene, unrelated to visibility — per-case remediation guidance, assisted explicit conversion, never silent); a **missing or stale diff report** (fingerprint mismatch, §6.4 — nobody upgrades blind, and nobody upgrades on last quarter's picture); and **unacknowledged service-account losses** (§6.6). `SCHEMA_VERSION` bumps with release 2 so pre-upgrade backups are rejected cleanly; no domain-loaded libraries exist at migration time (loading is Global-only today).
11. **UI shipped with this work**: the "default role" control, the folder-move access preview, the diff report (IAM audit) — and one line in the service-account creation flow noting that Team/Entity-backed owner resolution requires scoped grants (§4.3-6). `DOMAIN_MEMBERS` groups are excluded from SCIM group listings (they are derived, not provisionable).

## 7. Immediate fixes on main (independent of the rest)

### 7.1 Actor picker asymmetry (a confirmed bug)

Actors are the pointer records behind owner, reviewer, and assignee fields; each wraps a User, a Team, or an Entity. On main, the Users page and the actor pickers disagree: the Users list applies the general visibility rule, published mechanism included, while the actor path (`_get_actor_accessible_ids_by_perm`) checks role assignments only. Verified by test: a domain-scoped analyst sees a root-folder colleague on the Users page, but that same colleague is absent from the analyst's owner pickers.

The fix applies invariant 7: for the view prefix, the Actor delegate returns the actors whose underlying User, Team, or Entity is itself viewable by the general rule; the point check `_is_actor_accessible` delegates to `is_object_accessible` on the underlying object. Paradigm-proof: whatever the resolver returns tomorrow, actors follow.

### 7.2 GlobalSettings permission cleanup

Reads of UI configuration — feature flags, general settings, vulnerability SLA — become plain authenticated endpoints with no model permission. Every session needs them, so no role composition may be able to break them.

`view_globalsettings` and `change_globalsettings` are removed from the non-admin built-in roles. The permission returns to meaning "administers instance settings", checked as `is_access_allowed(perm, root)`. Sensitive rows (sso, infra-config, sec-intel-feeds) become admin-only through plain RBAC; the bolt-on `IsGlobalAdmin` guards stop being load-bearing, and the branch's "sensitive settings MUST have extra checks" comment is deleted — the invariant moves from a comment into the permission model.

### 7.3 Reader gaps (confirmed bugs)

`Reader`'s rule is *all view permissions*, minus the two Plumbing classes of §4.5. Three models break it today, none for a reason:

- **`OrganisationIssue`, `OrganisationObjective`** — `view_` appears only in `DOMAIN_MANAGER_PERMISSIONS_LIST` and `ADMINISTRATOR_PERMISSIONS_LIST` (`core/startup.py`), so Reader, Approver and Analyst cannot read either. Nothing distinguishes them from the organisational-context content every read-capable role already sees.
- **`LibraryFilteringLabel`** — held by Analyst, Domain Manager and Administrator but not Reader, while its sibling `FilteringLabel` is in Reader. It has its own endpoint and is a label catalogue; the asymmetry is an oversight.

Add the three view permissions to Reader (and to Approver and Analyst where they are also missing). All three are ordinary readable content, so after release 2 they sit in `Reader` and — for the two organisation models, which `main` published from the root — in `Reader Migration (legacy)` as well — which is also why they are flagged in the migration report as newly ambient (§6.6).

After release 2 this class of bug cannot recur in `Reader`, which is derived from the classification and therefore cannot omit a Content model. The operational roles keep hand-maintained permission lists, so for them the classification rule reports the discrepancy rather than fixing it.

### 7.4 Document templates are never ambient (a confirmed bug)

`DocumentTemplate` inherits `AbstractBaseModel`'s `is_published = False`, does not use `PublishInRootFolderMixin`, and `sync_document_templates` never sets the flag — so **every template, built-in or custom, is unpublished forever**. The published branch of `get_accessible_object_ids` runs for the model and matches nothing, leaving plain folder scoping, which for a domain-only user never contains the root's built-in templates.

The product compensates with a hardcoded literal — `Q(id__in=accessible_ids) | Q(builtin=True)` — in exactly two places, the template picker action (`doc_management/views.py`) and the document-create serializer (`doc_management/serializers.py`). That clause bypasses folder scoping *and* the permission check, and it is the only reason built-in templates are usable from a subdomain at all. Three consequences follow. The picker and `DocumentTemplateViewSet` (which has no such clause) return different sets for the same user, so a template is visible or invisible depending on which endpoint is asked — the same failure as §7.1, in a different subsystem. Global-authored *custom* templates are shared with nobody, which the code's own `TODO(revisit)` records as an open question rather than a bug. And template content is readable by any principal that reaches the endpoint, whatever its permissions.

**The fix is to use the mechanism rather than work around it.** `DocumentTemplate` declares `is_published = True` as its field default, a data migration backfills existing rows, and both `Q(builtin=True)` clauses and the TODO are deleted. Root built-ins then reach every holder of `view_documenttemplate` by the ordinary rule, and Global-authored custom templates reach domains for the same reason — one mechanism, no literal, and the two endpoints agree by construction. It makes an eighteenth model that redeclares the default to `True`, which is the right trade: one more instance of a pattern release 2 deletes wholesale, in exchange for deleting a bypass that release 2 would otherwise have to reason about.

**Nobody loses usable access.** `view_documenttemplate` is held by Reader, Approver, Analyst, Domain Manager and Administrator (`core/startup.py`); Third-party Respondent, Auditee and Technical Tester hold **no document or policy permission at all**, so removing the bypass takes away only template content they had no document surface on which to use it — a deliberate, release-noted tightening.

**Why release 1, and not later.** The practice map that freezes `Reader Migration (legacy)` (§4.2) is read *after* these fixes land. Publishing templates now means `DocumentTemplate` joins that role under the frozen rule, with no exception written for it, and templates never appear in release 2's newly-ambient disclosures. Deferring the fix would have forced a choice between writing an exception into a role whose whole value is that it has none, and leaving migrated tenants without built-in templates the moment the bypass was removed.

## 10. Conformance matrix (stable numbering for implementation tickets)

1. `readable(u, M)` ≡ `get_allowed_folder_ids(u, view_M)` — no other **folder-visibility decision path** exists (the overlay seam is empty). Nested serialization rides the host object's check (§4) and is tested as such, not counted as a second path.
2. No model carries visibility state (schema scan); RoleAssignment carries no lifecycle or provenance field.
3. Member-group existence ⇔ the folder has a default role, both directions; **at most one default-role row per folder** (write-time enforced); a `DOMAIN_MEMBERS` group is principal only of that row — any other reference is rejected at write time; the group stores no membership rows; enclave folders cannot have a default role.
4. Clearing the default role removes the group as a fully local act — no cross-folder effect, nothing dangles; ordinary role-assignment audit logging records the transition.
5. Changing a folder's default role replaces exactly that row; no other folder's assignments change.
6. Third-party, service-account, and deactivated principals never contribute to member groups; deactivation (including expiry-driven, via the scheduled task) takes effect on the first evaluation after commit; enclave exclusion holds for nested-enclave paths.
7. Multi-path membership: a user reachable through several effective source paths (direct, IdP-mapped, multiple groups) remains a member until the last path is gone — covered for direct removal, IdP mapping deletion, and `idp_groups` flag changes (a flag change affects effective assignment reach; RoleAssignment rows are unchanged).
8. Human role-assignment principals are exactly the two allowed kinds — **rejected at write time** (serializer/model); the boot check is a backstop that alarms and guides repair, never refuses boot on data findings; derived member groups never appear in their own derivation source.
9. A user in no managed group sees nothing.
10. `readable(Actor)` ≡ actors backed by readable(User) ∪ readable(Team) ∪ readable(Entity) (§7.1).
10a. Document templates resolve through the ordinary rule and nothing else: `DocumentTemplate` carries `is_published = True` by default with existing rows backfilled, **no code path selects templates by `builtin`** (static check), and the picker action, the create serializer and `DocumentTemplateViewSet` return the same set for the same user — root built-ins and Global-authored custom templates reach every holder of `view_documenttemplate`, and a principal holding none reaches no template (§7.4).
11. All normal User reads expose only the safe projection, for every caller; administrative metadata requires the administrative endpoint and permission. (Team and Entity are standard objects; no ambient grant exists for them.)
12. Default-role edits change visibility for exactly the target folder, create/replace/delete exactly the one projected row, and require the IAM-administration permission. The row's shape — one perimeter folder equal to the group's own, `is_recursive = False`, at most one per folder — is enforced by transactional domain validation under `Folder._lock_folder_tree()` and audited by a standing integrity check (§4.2 states why a `CHECK` constraint cannot span these relations); concurrent writers cannot create a second default role for a folder (tested under contention, not assumed).
12a. Default-role eligibility is revalidated through every many-to-many path — `permissions.add/remove/set/clear`, `perimeter_folders.add/remove/set/clear`, bulk and through-table writes, generic-register deletion — via canonical mutation services with `m2m_changed` backstops; a static check rejects bulk or through-table writes outside them. Concurrency tests for the folder-tree lock run on **PostgreSQL**, where `FOR UPDATE` exists.
13. A shelf sub-folder never inherits its parent's default-role assignment.
14. Folder move: the move and its closure update are atomic; membership is reflected by the next evaluation with no membership-specific step; an audit event lists affected member groups; enclave in/out and nested-enclave configurations covered.
15. Content flows and lifecycle features perform zero IAM writes (grep-able + integration test). Snapshot publication is outside these releases and is **not** asserted by this suite (row 30); what is tested is the interim placement-plus-grant workflow.
16. Custom-field form-schema projection: a host's form renders all applicable definitions for any user who can view the host.
17. Migration: release 2's migration sets `Reader Migration (legacy)` as the default role of exactly *Global ∪ {non-enclave folders holding ≥1 object of a legacy-list model and carrying no migration exclusion}*, atomically with the column drop; enclaves, excluded folders and folders with no such object get no row; the resulting rows are ordinary and removable, and a fresh install instead ships `Global - members × Reader Catalog`.
18. The diff report is exhaustive and per-model-family (gains and losses per principal, newly ambient placements flagged **and drillable to object identifiers**, `RiskAcceptance` losses named), is produced in release 1, and carries an **analysis fingerprint over its own findings** (per-folder posture decisions, per-principal deltas and drill-down lists, per-account loss sets, plus exclusions, `closure_epoch`, classification manifest and algorithm version — never the raw row corpus, and never the acknowledgements): anything that changes an assertion changes the hash, nothing else does. An acknowledgement is `(service_account, loss_set_hash, algorithm_version, disposition, actor, timestamp)` and is honoured while that account's recomputed loss set matches — an unrelated change elsewhere does not retire it; the migration halts on a missing report, on a fingerprint mismatch, on human direct-user role assignments (per-case remediation guidance, no silent conversion), and on unacknowledged service-account losses.
19. The migration creates no grants for service accounts; each account appears in the report with its permissions, its perimeter folders **and its computed losses** (what it reads today through the published branch and will not read afterwards); release 2 refuses to run while any such loss is unacknowledged; and a service account reads exactly what its own assignment names — nothing ambient.
20. Virtual membership: engine expansion ≡ independent reference recompute (property test); deduplicated; query plans tested on deep trees and many-group users; ≤1 additional indexed query per principal resolution; request-scoped memoization permitted, cross-request caching absent initially.
21. The member-group kind discriminator is immutable and the sole trigger of special behavior; `DOMAIN_MEMBERS` groups are created/deleted only via the default-role transitions; folder binding never reassignable; excluded from SCIM group listings.
22. No code path joins the raw membership table for member groups — all consumers use the canonical expansion helper (static check + integration test).
23. Effective group membership (direct ∪ IdP-mapped) is one shared relation consumed by principal resolution and the evaluator — a user reachable only via an IdP mapping appears exactly where a directly-added member would.
24. Closure integrity: a static CI check rejects bulk writes to `parent_folder`; `check_iam_closure` runs after every migration (sender-filtered or otherwise guarded — `post_migrate` fires per app and again after `flush`), at the end of restore, first in report generation, first in release 2's migration, and on demand from the audit page; a repair bumps `closure_epoch`, invalidating reports drawn against the old topology; it repairs under `Folder._lock_folder_tree()` in one transaction, is idempotent under concurrent invocation, and records each finding with counts, folder ids and the direction of the access change, surfaced on the audit page. It never refuses boot. The membership property test additionally fails against a closure-free reference recompute.
24a. Migration protocol: release 2 ships **two migration files** — a non-atomic preflight that repairs the closure in a committed transaction and aborts on change with `closure_epoch` preserved (so the repair-invalidate-abort cycle cannot repeat indefinitely), and an atomic destructive migration that begins with a **detect-only** closure check (abort, never repair, if corrupt), then validates the report's findings fingerprint and evaluates every gate, verifies the cheap change detector (row counts, `max(updated_at)` watermarks, `closure_epoch`) inside the transaction rather than rescanning, then creates the assignments and drops the columns, committing all or nothing — the transaction covering database changes only, with legacy-code removal and `SCHEMA_VERSION` shipping in the same deployment rather than the same transaction; a gate failure there leaves the migration unrecorded and the preflight's work intact; the destructive run requires the application down or read-only, or a lock honoured by every mutation path feeding the fingerprint.
24b. Simulator fidelity: for a sampled cross-product of principals and models on a representative dataset, the old-reach simulator's answer equals what the live `main` published-branch code path returns; the test runs in release 1, while that code still exists to compare against.
24c. The classification manifest covers every model in the codebase — no model is absent, and every Plumbing entry names its class; both built-in roles are generated from the manifest rather than hand-maintained, and §6.2's predicate reads the same manifest.
25. Every new securable model carries a classification — Catalog, Content, or Plumbing-with-a-named-class (CI check); classification by omission fails the build. `Reader Catalog` contains exactly the Catalog models and `Reader` exactly Catalog ∪ Content, both **generated** from the manifest rather than hand-maintained; `Reader Migration (legacy)` is frozen and gains nothing. The Plumbing set must match the §4.5 inventory exactly, so extending it requires editing that list — a reviewer sees every addition.
26. Built-in roles reject tenant edits; product changes to their permission lists contain only new-in-release models or carry an access-migration marker.
27. The diff report is reachable only with `view_role` at the root folder — enforced by the audit surface itself on the API and by a route guard, never inherited from the Experimental hub's link gating (`/experimental/*` carries no server-side guard); a domain-scoped grant of the same permission does not open it.
28. A role is eligible as a default role only if it contains `view_*` permissions exclusively and no `Plumbing` permission; the rule is enforced when the default is set **and** when a role already used as a default is edited (a role edit that would break it is rejected, not silently applied).
29. Migration exclusions live outside IAM, are written only through the audit tool with actor and reason, are honoured by the §6.2 predicate, **cannot target Global** (rejected at write time), and expire with the migration — no exclusion ever grants or revokes anything by itself.
30. No release of this project ships snapshot publication (§4.3-7): the conformance suite asserts only that lifecycle code performs zero IAM writes, and the interim placement-plus-grant workflow is what the document tests describe.
31. `makemigrations --check` clean; write-time validators active; boot backstops green.

## 11. Release plan *(planning — evolves with implementation)*

**Release 1 creates no RoleAssignment, no default role and no member group.** Every assignment this project introduces is materialized by release 2's migration, atomically with the drop. It does add permissions to existing roles (§7.3), so it is not access-neutral — see §6.1.

### 11.1 Release 1 — fixes + IAM audit

1. **Actor picker asymmetry fix (§7.1).** Regression test = the verified scenario (the root-folder colleague appears in owner pickers exactly iff the Users page shows them). Paradigm-proof: when release 2 changes what the resolver returns, actors follow with no further edit.
2. **GlobalSettings permission cleanup (§7.2).** The SSO `IsGlobalAdmin` guard is removed **in this same release** (§8's "until 7.2 lands" is satisfied here), and the branch's "extra checks" comment goes with it. Release-note side effect: the Extra → Experimental **link** is gated by the `experimental` feature flag *and* by `permissions: ['change_globalsettings']` (`navData.ts`), so after §7.2 that entry narrows to global administrators. **Link gating is not access control, though**: `/experimental/*` has no server-side route guard today — only the `(internal)` layout, which merely redirects third parties — so a direct request reaches the page. The audit surface therefore **enforces its own gate**: `is_access_allowed(view_role, root folder)` on the API, with a matching route guard so a non-administrator gets a clean refusal instead of an empty page, and `view_role` on the nav card so the link and the enforcement agree. `view_role` is a Plumbing permission (§4.5 tier 2) held today only by `ADMINISTRATOR`; naming an existing administrator-surface permission avoids inventing one, and checking it **at the root folder** means a domain-scoped custom role that includes it cannot reach the tool. (`view_roleassignment` would read better still, but no role holds it today — gating on it would lock everyone out, administrators included.) That gate, not the placement, is what satisfies conformance row 27.
3. **Reader gap fix (§7.3).** Add `view_organisationissue`, `view_organisationobjective` and `view_libraryfilteringlabel` to Reader (and to Approver/Analyst where missing) — a few lines in `core/startup.py` plus a role re-sync, independent of everything else, and it puts release 2's classification on a correct baseline.
4. **Document-template publication fix (§7.4).** `is_published = True` as `DocumentTemplate`'s field default, a data migration backfilling existing rows, and the deletion of both `Q(builtin=True)` clauses and the `TODO(revisit)` beside them. Regression test: a domain-scoped reader gets identical template sets from the picker action, the create serializer and `DocumentTemplateViewSet`, root built-ins included; a principal without `view_documenttemplate` gets none from any of the three. Like item 3, this must land before the practice map is read.
5. **The IAM audit tool — Extra → Experimental, kept after the migration.**
   - **Placement:** a new card and subroute on the existing experimental hub (`/experimental/iam-audit`); sidebar section `extra` → entry `experimental` — no new navigation surface. Backend: the headless management command (for self-hosted and CI) plus the API the page consumes.
   - **Two foundations ship here before anything can use them:** the **one-time classification manifest** (§4.2 rule 0 — every existing model sorted into Assessment / Content / Plumbing, the source both built-in roles and the migration predicate are generated from), and the **old-reach simulator** below. Neither is a by-product of another task; both are on the critical path for release 2.
   - **The largest build item in this release is the old-reach simulator**, and it is easy to under-scope. What earlier drafts dropped was the *compatibility-assignment generator* and the *proposal workflow* — never the ability to compute what `main` exposes today. The service-account gate needs per-account old reach, and the human diff needs the same engine across every principal: a faithful evaluation of the published branch (per-model conditioning, the root mixin, policy-backed documents, enclave exclusion) against the live tenant. One engine serves both; scope release 1 around it — and give it the **differential fidelity test** of §6.3, which can only be written while `main`'s code is still there to compare against.
   - **Panes (the §6.4 diff report, per tenant):** what the posture will change, **per folder and per model family** — gains and losses per principal, with the **newly ambient placements flagged** as the genuinely new access (root-only families applied to domains; standalone documents; §6.4); human direct-user role assignments with per-case remediation guidance and the **assisted, explicit, per-row conversion tool**; the service-account inventory with each account's grants (§6.6); which folders will receive the posture and which will not (enclave, excluded, or holding no object of a legacy-list model).
   - **Disclosure, not an approval gate:** the report exists so nobody upgrades blind, and release 2 halts on a missing report, a **stale fingerprint**, unresolved direct-user rows or unacknowledged service-account losses — but it blocks on no per-tenant *preference*. An operator who wants a folder kept out writes a **migration exclusion** here (§6.4); administrators tighten afterwards with one dropdown.
   - **Three migration artifacts for release 2**, all written and displayed by this tool: the **report with its analysis fingerprint**; the **exclusion records** (folder id, actor, reason — never Global); and the **service-account acknowledgements** (`account, loss_set_hash, analysis_fingerprint, disposition, actor, timestamp`). One is an analysis, two are operator decisions; release 2 consumes all three.
   - **Includes `manage.py check_iam_closure`** — full detect-repair-record from release 1 (§4.1): the static bulk-write CI check, the `post_migrate` and post-restore invocations, run-now from this page, and findings displayed here with counts, folder ids and the direction of the access change. It never gates ordinary startup; release 2 uses it explicitly as a migration precondition (§6.9). Otherwise it is hygiene for a table every recursive assignment already depends on.
   - **Permanent life.** The tool is not migration scaffolding: after release 2 it remains as the standing IAM audit — closure integrity, principal-kind backstop findings (invariant 4), existence-binding and self-row-only checks (rows 3–5), member-group overview, custom-role catalog-drift report (§4.2) — absorbing today's ad-hoc `audit_visibility` command and the fleet scan script. Migration-specific panes archive to a historical report. Promotion out of Experimental is a later product decision.
6. **Write-time validator: human direct-user principals.** Only this one in release 1 — its subject exists today; the kind-discriminator, lifecycle and single-value validators guard objects that don't exist until release 2 and ship with them. *(Strikeable if release 1 must stay strictly fixes-plus-audit: without it, rows created after cleanup simply re-halt release 2's migration — safe, but a second remediation loop.)*
7. **Independent branch salvage (§8 Keep):** the migration-graph repairs and the GlobalSettings attribute-name fix.

**Explicitly not in release 1:** no member-group machinery, no `DOMAIN_MEMBERS` kind, no built-in reader roles, no folder control, no visibility schema change, **no new RoleAssignment, member group or default role** — and the resolver code that reads `is_published` is untouched. The flag's *schema* is touched once, narrowly: §7.4 alters `DocumentTemplate`'s field default and backfills it, which changes data inside the existing mechanism rather than changing the mechanism. Release 1 is not access-neutral and does not claim to be (effective access moves through the §7 fixes).

**Cross-cutting:** i18n keys for the new page and nav card (EN + FR); EE overlay parity for any forked file touched (notably the `(app)` layout pair).

### 11.2 Release 2 — the big migration

1. **Machinery (additive, same release as the drop):** the member-group lifecycle bound to the default role (§4.1); the `DOMAIN_MEMBERS` kind with its write-time validators (kind immutability, default-role-transition-only lifecycle, self-row-only principals, folder never reassignable) and the enclave prohibition (row 3); the **virtual membership evaluator** with its expansion property test, query-plan tests, and the normative performance budget (row 20); the two built-in roles with the new-model classification CI rule (rows 25–26); the single-valued **"default role"** folder control under the IAM-administration gate (row 12); the universal **safe User projection** + administrative endpoint split (row 11); the custom-field **form-schema projection** (row 16); SCIM exclusion of `DOMAIN_MEMBERS` (row 21). `check_iam_closure` ships complete in release 1 (§4.1); release 2 only calls it.
2. **The destructive migration — two migration files** (§6.9). **Preflight** (`atomic = False`): `check_iam_closure` validates and repairs in a committed transaction; if it changed anything it bumps `closure_epoch` and aborts with "regenerate the IAM report", the epoch surviving the abort; a later clean run records it as applied. **Destructive** (`atomic = True`), with the application down or read-only: at the start of the transaction, a **detect-only closure check** (abort with repair-and-regenerate instructions if corrupt — never repair here), then the findings-fingerprint validation, the gates (row 18: missing or stale report, human direct-user rows, unmatched service-account acknowledgements), then the roles, member groups and default-role rows, then the 158 column drops — committed together or not at all. **Code removal is not in the transaction**: the legacy resolver branches and the per-model `default=True` writers go with the same deployment, and the release sets `SCHEMA_VERSION = 3` as a code constant. Repairing after the gates would validate a report against a topology that no longer exists; repairing inside the destructive transaction would roll the repair back on every halt, forever.
3. **Branch pivot (§8):** the current branch contributes its column-drop migrations and cleanups to release 2; its §8 **Remove** list never ships in any release.
4. **Post-migration:** the IAM audit tool switches to standing mode; the diff report is retained as the record of what the upgrade changed.

**Out of scope for this plan:** threat-model and test-case decomposition; ticket-level breakdown (the conformance rows in §10 are the stable ticket references).

## 12. Design principles distilled

1. **One primitive.** If a capability can be expressed as groups, roles, and assignments, it must be. The count of visibility-specific mechanisms in this design is zero, and RoleAssignment has no lifecycle states.
2. **Defaults are data; existence is a decision.** The default role is explicit, removable administration: setting it creates the folder's member group, clearing it removes the group, and ordinary audit records every transition.
3. **Invariants live in structure** — write-time validators first, boot checks and integrity commands as detection backstops, conformance tests always — never in comments, save hooks, or conventions.
4. **Derived semantics must be independently verifiable**: property-test the virtual expansion against a reference recomputation, enforce closure integrity in production, and keep no separately synchronized membership state.
5. **Identity gates ambience; position gates enclaves**: third parties, machine principals, and deactivated accounts get exactly their explicit grants; enclave exclusion is a property of paths, not of people.
6. **Components follow roots; delegates follow delegatees; nested content rides its host's check.**
7. **Placement scopes reference material; the folder default role shares work at folder granularity (below it, direct grants on the holding sub-folder); lifecycles act by placement; IAM is written only by administrators and the install/upgrade seeding.**
8. **When an object seems to need special visibility treatment, first check whether a permission is doing double duty** (the GlobalSettings lesson). When a principal seems to need special directory treatment, first check whether it is simply a standard object (the Team/Entity lesson).
9. **Capability-changing steps are measured exhaustively and disclosed before they happen** — the measurement ships one release ahead of the change, per folder and per model family, gains and losses alike; what is *claimed* never exceeds what was measured.
10. **Restore the intent, not the accident.** When shipped behavior has drifted from the design it was meant to express, migrate to the design and disclose the difference — and encode the intent where it cannot drift again (a role derived from a build-checked classification, never per-model boilerplate). Keep dependants working by granting them what they need, not by replaying what they happened to see; and weigh any compatibility exercise against the age and size of what depends on it.
